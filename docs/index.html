Where have climate extremes changed tree growth, and why?

Climate change has made hot growing seasons far more frequent. The map shows how much these additional extreme years have changed annual growth at each site, estimated from the rings, and the factor that a theory-guided neural network learned to limit growth there.

How the change is estimated

For each site, a chronology of size-detrended growth is regressed on hot years, dry years and severe winters (thresholds from its 1902–1960 climate) and the following year. The response is multiplied by the change in their frequency between the observed and the ISIMIP3a counterfactual climate in 1990–2019. No neural network is used for this estimate.

The theory-guided network

T+S builds a size-dependent growth potential (Richards function, metabolic scaling) and Liebig’s principle of limiting factors into its structure: in each month growth is set by the lower of a temperature and a soil-moisture response. A neural network predicts their site-specific parameters. The limiting factor shown here is the share of the growing season in which temperature, rather than moisture, limits growth.

Read with care

Values describe trees of the tree-ring archive, which over-represents climate-sensitive sites and North America and Europe; they are not forest productivity or the carbon sink. The estimate captures only change acting through seasonal extremes. Site values are noisy; group means and intervals are the robust result. No raw ring-width data are included.
